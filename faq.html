WiFi, Mesh & Routers

What is mesh WiFi?

Mesh WiFi uses more than one WiFi point around your property. Instead of relying on one router in one corner of the house, mesh units work together to spread coverage more evenly.

It is useful for larger homes, thick walls, loft rooms, extensions, garden rooms and properties where the WiFi drops out in certain rooms.

Is mesh WiFi better than a WiFi booster?

Usually, yes. Basic plug-in boosters can repeat a weak signal and often reduce speed. A proper mesh system is normally more stable and easier to move around the property with a device, The units are designed to work together.

Best results often come from wiring mesh access points with Ethernet where possible.
Will mesh WiFi make my internet faster?

Mesh WiFi cannot make your broadband line faster than the speed coming into the property, but it can help you actually receive that speed in more rooms.

If your broadband is 500 Mbps but your upstairs room only gets 20 Mbps over poor WiFi, a better WiFi setup can make a huge difference.

Why are internet provider routers often poor compared with purchased routers?

ISP (internet service providers) supplied routers are usually made to be cheap, simple and good enough for average use. They often struggle when a property has lots of devices, thick walls, smart TVs, CCTV, gaming consoles, home working equipment and smart home devices.

A good purchased router or access point system can offer:

  • Better WiFi range
  • More stable connections
  • Improved handling of multiple devices
  • Better parental controls and guest networks
  • More advanced settings and security options
What is the difference between a router and an access point?

A router manages your network and connects your devices to the internet. An access point mainly provides WiFi coverage and is usually connected back to the router by Ethernet cable.

For the best coverage, many properties benefit from one main router and one or more properly positioned access points.

Can you improve WiFi in a garden room or outbuilding?

Yes. The best option is usually to run a proper data cable to the garden room and install an access point inside. This is normally much more reliable than trying to stretch WiFi from the house.

Network Cabling

What is the difference between Cat5, Cat5e, Cat6, Cat6A, Cat7 and Cat8?

These are different categories of network cable. The higher categories can support better performance, but the right choice depends on the job.

  • Cat5: Older cable. Generally not recommended for new installs.
  • Cat5e: Common for gigabit networking and still fine for many basic jobs.
  • Cat6: A strong choice for modern homes and small businesses. Better performance and less interference than Cat5e.
  • Cat6A: Better for 10Gbps networks and longer high-speed runs.
  • Cat6e: Often used as a marketing term. Cat6A is the proper recognised upgrade from Cat6.
  • Cat7: Shielded cable, but not usually necessary for normal domestic or small business installs.
  • Cat8: Mainly for short specialist data centre connections, not typical home cabling.
For most new home and small business installs, Cat6 or Cat6A is normally the sensible choice.
Is Ethernet better than WiFi?

For fixed devices, yes. Ethernet is usually faster, more stable and lower latency than WiFi. It is ideal for TVs, gaming consoles, office desks, CCTV systems, access points and media equipment.

WiFi is still useful for phones, tablets and portable devices, but important equipment is often better wired where possible.

What is network cabinet rationalisation?

It means tidying, organising and correcting a messy network cabinet or comms area. This can include labelling cables, removing unused equipment, improving patching, checking faults and making the setup easier to maintain.

Can you find faults in network cables?

Yes. We can help trace and test network cabling faults, poor terminations, damaged cables, unreliable ports and messy patch panels.

Do I need fibre optic cabling?

Most homes and small businesses do not need fibre inside the property, but it can be useful for longer runs, links between buildings, high-speed networks or areas where electrical interference is a concern.

Wired CCTV is usually more stable because it does not rely on WiFi signal strength. Wireless cameras can be useful in some situations, but wired systems are often better for reliability and long-term use.
